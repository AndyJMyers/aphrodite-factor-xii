# Chapter I
## The Golden Apple: Make Them Want What You Have

The trouble began, as trouble so often does, with a wedding.

Not the wedding itself, which was perfectly respectable as these things go: a sea-goddess, a mortal king, half the gods of Olympus, tables groaning under roast lamb, wine poured by the amphora, and enough gold ornament to finance a small war.

No, the trouble began with the guest list.

Eris, goddess of discord, had not been invited.

“An understandable administrative choice,” said Penelope.

Dionysus, reclining beneath a fig tree with a cup of wine and the rather pleased expression of a god who had not himself organised the party, shook his head.

“Rookie error. If you do not invite Discord to a wedding, she does not stay at home and take up embroidery. She comes anyway, but without the table plan.”

Penelope looked towards the water. Below them, the last guests of the symposium were making their uncertain way down towards the harbour. One of them had lost a sandal, another a lyric, and a third had gained an opinion about naval policy that he would regret by morning.

“Eris came,” she said, “bearing an apple.”

“A very good apple.”

“A golden apple.”

“Better still.”

“And on it she had written: *For the fairest*.”

Dionysus raised his cup.

“Three words. Not a lecture, not a poem, not even a particularly expensive gift. Three words, and the whole business went mad.”

That was the apple’s genius. Eris did not give it to Hera, Athena or Aphrodite. Had she done so, there would have been sulking, perhaps a thunderbolt, and a long note sent through the proper divine channels.

Instead she placed it where all three could see it.

Then she left.

For anyone who has not spent much time among gods, this may sound reckless. It was. Hera wanted it because she was queen of heaven and queens do not care to discover that the heavens are considering an alternative. Athena wanted it because she was wise, formidable, armoured, and not so wise that she was indifferent to being thought beautiful. Aphrodite wanted it because she was Aphrodite, and if beauty could be said to have a chief executive, it was she.

Zeus, who was perfectly capable of throwing mountains but less eager to adjudicate an argument between three women, passed the task to Paris of Troy.

“Also a sound administrative decision,” said Dionysus.

“No,” said Penelope. “A cowardly one.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Paris was young, handsome, bored, and in possession of precisely the sort of confidence found in men who have not yet had enough responsibility placed upon them. He was offered power by Hera, military glory and wisdom by Athena, and the love of the most beautiful woman in the world by Aphrodite.

He chose Aphrodite.

“Of course he did,” said Dionysus. “He was a young man.”

“He was a fool.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive either.”

## The Apple Is Not the Prize

Here is the first lesson of the golden apple, and it is a better one than Paris managed to learn.

**What people desire is often not the thing itself, but the meaning attached to possessing it.**

The apple was useless. You could not eat it, spend it, wear it, ride it, turn it into a pleasant little estate overlooking the sea, or even use it to prop open a door. But it represented a judgement: *you are the one above the others*.

And people will do extraordinary things for that judgement.

They will purchase watches they cannot read properly, houses they cannot afford to heat, cars too wide for the roads they live on, handbags with the practical capacity of a small mollusc, and drinks served in bottles large enough to require two hands and a minor ceremony.

They will go to parties they dislike in the hope of being seen at them. They will enter arguments they do not understand because somebody implied their intelligence. They will remain in unhappy romances because leaving would look like losing.

The golden apple does not feed hunger. It feeds the fear of being overlooked.

“Which is why,” said Dionysus, “you should always bring a little more wine than people expect.”

Penelope looked at him.

“Your solution to every psychological problem is wine.”

“Not every problem. Some require dancing.”

The point stands. If you wish to be valued, do not simply possess something. Make it clear, without announcing it, that it has been chosen.

You know a place that is good. You can do a thing that most people cannot. You have a way of making an evening work. You are calm when everyone else flaps. You know when a gift is right, a compliment is earned, an introduction is useful, and a second bottle is a mistake.

These are your apples.

Not the sort to throw into a room and let mayhem ensue—although there is a time and place for that—but the sort that makes people think, *How did they know that? How do they make it look so easy? Why was I not already invited?*

That last question is the beginning of leverage.

## Never Be Available in a Barrel

The second lesson is more difficult.

Your apple must not be everywhere.

A thing which is always available, always offered, always discussed, always forced upon people, loses force. It is no longer a prize. It is a free leaflet at the harbour.

This does not mean becoming aloof, obscure, flaky, rude, or one of those monstrous people who says, “I’m rather hard to get hold of,” while holding a device capable of receiving messages from the other side of the earth.

It means having standards.

You do not attend every gathering. You do not tell every story you possess. You do not share every achievement before it has settled into something real. You do not offer every favour to every person at every hour. You do not fling open the gates of your private life simply because somebody has been agreeable over lunch.

“Ah,” said Dionysus. “Mystery.”

“Discernment,” said Penelope.

“Same wine, different amphora.”

No, they are not quite the same.

Mystery is a mist. Discernment is a gate.

Mystery says, “Nobody knows where I am or what I am doing.” This may be alluring for a week. It becomes tiresome when you are meant to be at dinner and everyone is eating cold olives at midnight.

Discernment says, “I have a life, preferences, loyalties, work, pleasures and limits. If I give you my time, it means something.”

That is the thing people want.

Not universal access. Chosen access.

The lover who is available at every hour quickly becomes a service. The friend who says yes to every invitation becomes furniture. The colleague who fixes every crisis becomes an appliance. And an appliance is rarely seduced, consulted or toasted.

“Speak for yourself,” said Dionysus. “I have seen what happens to a well-kept wine press after harvest.”

Penelope ignored him.

Make yourself available enough to be trusted, but not so available that your attention ceases to have a price. And by price, we do not mean coin. We mean reciprocity, thought, courtesy, effort, and the ordinary human grace of not assuming that another person’s life is simply an annexe to one’s own.

## Do Not Throw the Apple at Their Head

There is, naturally, a bad version of this technique.

The bad version is jealousy.

A man makes a show of flirting with somebody else in the hope of provoking the person he actually wants. A woman turns every dinner into an inventory of more glamorous invitations. A friend repeatedly reminds you of all the fascinating people they know, while somehow failing ever to introduce you to one.

This is not the golden apple. This is leaving a dead fish in somebody’s sandals.

It may produce a reaction. Many bad strategies do. Setting fire to a curtain produces an immediate response from a room. That does not make it a desirable way to begin a relationship.

The golden apple creates desire by making value visible. Jealousy creates desire by making people feel diminished.

One can begin a romance with either. Only one has a reasonable chance of surviving breakfast.

“Some romances are not intended to survive breakfast,” said Dionysus.

“Then they should at least survive the bill.”

This is the difference between seduction and social arson.

If you are beautiful, clever, talented, rich, powerful, funny, well-connected, charming, or capable of producing smoked fish and excellent bread from an apparently empty kitchen, good for you. Let people notice. Do not make them watch you notice yourself.

The best display is incidental.

The good host does not say, “Observe my excellent gathering.” People realise it halfway through the evening, when they find themselves happier than expected and unable to identify the moment it happened.

The skilled person does not say, “I am very skilled.” They fix the thing, arrange the thing, explain the thing, or make the thing appear. Then they return to their drink.

The attractive person does not need to be the loudest person in the room. Indeed, once you are good-looking enough, being a little less eager than everybody else is usually a substantial improvement.

“You see?” said Dionysus. “At last we reach the important material.”

“We were already there.”

## The Paris Problem

Poor Paris is a warning to us all.

He was handed a choice between power, wisdom, glory and the most beautiful woman in the world. He selected the last, with consequences involving a ruined city, a great many dead Greeks and Trojans, an enormous wooden horse, and at least one marriage that could have benefited from better boundaries.

His error was not desiring Aphrodite’s gift. That is perfectly understandable. His error was believing that the apple had no cost.

Every desirable thing has a cost.

A more beautiful lover may bring greater competition. A more exciting party may lead to a worse morning. A more influential friend may expect more of you. A higher social position may come with more people waiting to see you fall from it.

This is not an argument against pleasure, beauty, adventure, or favourable comparison. It is an argument for knowing the price before you leave the shop.

Penelope had waited twenty years for Odysseus to come home, ten of them occupied by a war which was largely the result of Paris choosing a prize without reading the small inscription beneath it.

*Terms and conditions apply. Ships may burn. Families may be complicated.*

“Still,” said Dionysus, “Helen was very beautiful.”

“Dionysus.”

“I am only saying that men do not sail across the wine-dark sea for administrative efficiency.”

“No. They sail because they are susceptible to a story in which they are the hero.”

“And women?”

“Women sail for reasons of their own. You would understand more if you occasionally listened before refilling their cups.”

Dionysus thought about this.

Then he refilled Penelope’s cup.

## Your First Apple

This week, identify one thing you possess that is actually worth having around.

Not a boast. Not an aspiration. Not the version of you that appears in the best photograph taken during the best holiday of 2019.

Something real.

Perhaps it is your taste. Perhaps it is your steadiness. Perhaps you make people laugh. Perhaps you know a good route through a difficult city, a good tradesman, a good book, a good wine, a good way through an argument, or a good place to sit when everyone has talked too much.

Perhaps you can carry heavy objects without complaint.

This last gift has founded more durable friendships than philosophy.

Now make your apple visible once, naturally and without a trumpet.

Offer the excellent recommendation. Bring the good bottle. Make the useful introduction. Host the small supper. Solve the annoying problem. Wear the jacket. Tell the story. Do the thing you do well.

Then stop.

Leave space for people to recognise the value themselves.

If they do, they will remember you. If they remember you, they will speak of you when you are absent. If they speak of you favourably when you are absent, your reputation has begun working while you are asleep.

That is the nearest ordinary mortals come to immortality.

Or, at least, to being invited again.
